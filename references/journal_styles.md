# Journal-Specific Style Reference
# AER · QJE · JPE · Econometrica · MS · JFE · JEEA · REStud · JRS

Apply the rules in the relevant section when the user specifies a target journal.
Rules are additive: start from general top-journal standards (SKILL.md) and layer these on top.

---

## [AER] — American Economic Review

**Signature features:**
- Abstract: ≤ 150 words; structured but not labeled (no "Objective:", "Methods:" headers)
- Tone: authoritative, economical; the journal of record—every sentence earns its place
- Introduction: typically 3–5 pages; the "Our paper does X, Y, Z" contribution list is conventional but should be brief
- Contribution framing: explicit "relative to [prior paper]" comparisons mandatory
- Results presentation: lead with the economic magnitude, not merely significance
- Avoid: rhetorical questions in abstracts; "We believe..."; informal hedges

**Key stylistic markers:**
- "We study / examine / estimate / document" — not "investigate" (QJE preferred) or "analyze" (too generic)
- Footnotes used heavily for caveats, robustness qualifications, and data details
- Tables follow strict AER formatting: column headers bold, significance stars footnoted, standard errors in parentheses
- "We find that X increases Y by Z percent" (quantify first, qualify after)

**Sentence structure:**
- Subject–verb–object; minimize passive voice except in data/methods sections
- Long sentences permitted only if grammatically clean and logically tight
- Never start a paragraph with "However," without prior setup in the paragraph above

## [QJE] — Quarterly Journal of Economics

**Signature features:**
- Abstract: ≤ 200 words; punch hard on the main finding in sentence 1–2
- Introduction: ambitious scope; the "big picture" framing is stronger than AER
- Contribution: clearly stated paragraph, often with 3 numbered contributions
- Tone: confident, occasionally bold; QJE accepts more dramatic empirical claims when well-identified
- Data: "novel dataset" framing common; emphasize institutional setting

**Key stylistic markers:**
- "We provide evidence that..." (QJE's preferred hedge for strong causal claims)
- "This paper is the first to..." — only acceptable when demonstrably true
- "The key insight is..." — acceptable in introduction
- Mechanism section: required for any reduced-form paper; "This pattern is consistent with X because..."
- "We document / uncover / establish" preferred over "show"

**Sentence structure:**
- More rhetorical variation allowed than AER
- Can open with motivating anecdote or policy context paragraph
- Explicit roadmap paragraph at end of introduction: "The remainder of the paper is organized as follows..."

## [JPE] — Journal of Political Economy

**Signature features:**
- Abstract: shorter, often ≤ 100 words; extremely direct
- Introduction: theory–empirics integration expected; if empirical, must motivate via theory
- Tone: intellectual gravitas; JPE expects papers to make a lasting conceptual contribution
- Theoretical papers: proposition–proof format; lemmas labeled and cited within
- Empirical papers: model first, then estimation; structural preferred over pure reduced-form

**Key stylistic markers:**
- Formal precision in claims; avoid hedging theoretical results
- "The model predicts X; the data confirm this prediction" — theory-consistent framing
- Structural estimation: "We estimate the model using [method] to recover structural parameter θ"
- "The welfare implications of this result are..." — JPE expects welfare-relevant conclusions

## [ECTA] — Econometrica

**Signature features:**
- Theory-heavy; mathematical exposition must be self-contained
- Every assumption numbered and explicitly stated (Assumption 1, 2, ...)
- Propositions, Lemmas, Theorems, Corollaries all labeled and cross-referenced
- Abstract: ≤ 150 words; state main theoretical result and, if applicable, empirical application
- Tone: strictly formal; no rhetorical devices

**Key stylistic markers:**
- "Under Assumption [N], Proposition [M] establishes that..."
- "The equilibrium is unique and characterized by..."
- Notation must be defined at first use; maintain a notational consistency table internally
- Empirical sections labeled "Empirical Application" or "Quantitative Analysis" — not a standalone paper
- Proofs: either in main text (short) or Appendix (long); cross-reference throughout

**Prohibited patterns:**
- Colloquial framing in main text
- Missing proof sketches for non-trivial claims
- Undefined notation

## [MS] — Management Science

**Signature features:**
- Broader audience: strategy, operations, finance, IS, marketing, OB — adjust vocabulary accordingly
- Abstract: ≤ 150 words; must include managerial implications
- Introduction: "This paper contributes to the literature on [X] by..." structure conventional
- Tone: rigorous but accessible; avoid highly technical notation in prose sections
- Empirical section: emphasize context (industry, firm, platform) more than AER/QJE

**Key stylistic markers:**
- "Our findings have implications for managers seeking to..."
- "We contribute to [stream 1], [stream 2], and [stream 3] by..."
- Mechanism analysis: explicitly labeled "Mechanism" or "Why" subsection
- "Consistent with [theory], we find that..." — theory-linking framing
- Policy / managerial takeaway section often expected

**MS-specific constructions:**
- "We exploit the [event/regulation/discontinuity] as a source of plausibly exogenous variation"
- "Our identification relies on the assumption that [exclusion restriction / parallel trends / continuity]"
- Tables: include both OLS and IV / FE columns for comparison

## [JFE] — Journal of Financial Economics

**Signature features:**
- Asset pricing, corporate finance, banking, VC/PE focus
- Abstract: ≤ 150 words; key result quantified (e.g., "reduces leverage by 5 percentage points")
- Introduction: motivating stylized fact or institutional detail paragraph is standard
- Tone: technically rigorous; factor model / event study / structural model framing expected
- Event studies: CAPM or 3-factor / 5-factor abnormal returns; CAR must be explained

**Key stylistic markers:**
- "Consistent with the [agency / information asymmetry / market frictions] hypothesis..."
- "Firms in the top quintile of [X] earn abnormal returns of Y bps per month"
- "We control for firm fixed effects, year fixed effects, and [firm characteristics]"
- Alternative hypotheses must be explicitly stated and tested / ruled out

**Quantification norms:**
- Always report economic magnitudes in addition to statistical significance
- "A one-standard-deviation increase in X is associated with a Y-basis-point increase in yield spread"

## [JEEA] — Journal of the European Economic Association

**Signature features:**
- Strong preference for clean identification + European / developing-country institutional settings
- Abstract: 150–200 words; state the identification strategy explicitly
- Tone: similar to AER but slightly more tolerant of contextual framing
- Policy relevance: frequently expected; welfare / distributional analysis valued

**Key stylistic markers:**
- "We use [natural experiment / policy variation] in [country/region] to identify..."
- Distributional results: "Effects are concentrated among [subgroup]..."
- "Our results are relevant for the ongoing policy debate on..."

---

## [REStud] — Review of Economic Studies

**Signature features:**
- Strong theory and structural-empirical integration; flagship for economic theory in Europe
- Abstract: ≤ 150 words; theoretical results stated formally if possible
- Tone: similar to Econometrica for theory papers; AER-adjacent for empirical
- Young-researcher friendly: longer "related literature" engagement expected

**Key stylistic markers:**
- "We characterize the optimal [mechanism / contract / policy] as a function of..."
- "The key friction is [X]; removing it would lead to [Y]"
- "We establish existence and uniqueness of equilibrium under Assumptions 1–3"
- Empirical papers: "The estimates are stable across a range of specifications (Tables [X]–[Y])"

## [JRS] — Journal of Rural Studies

> **Source paper**: Liu, W. & Hu, Z. (2025). *Effect of Internet usage on nonfarm employment participation and quality: Evidence from China.* Journal of Rural Studies, 120, 103882.
> This section distills the stylistic conventions observed in this published JRS paper. It serves as a personalized language style option for the current user, whose own research engages with similar themes (rural development, causal inference, China-focused empirical work). Activate with `[JRS]` or `[Journal of Rural Studies]`.

**Journal profile:**
- Interdisciplinary social science journal (rural geography, sociology, development studies)
- Empirical focus; quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods accepted
- Strong China/developing-country presence
- More accessible than pure economics journals (AER/QJE); less mathematical density
- Policy-relevant framing expected; international audience assumed

### Signature Features

**Tone & accessibility:**
- Academic but not forbiddingly technical; a well-informed non-specialist should be able to follow all prose sections
- Avoid dense econometric terminology in the main narrative; relegate technical details to footnotes or appendix
- Occasional use of metaphors permitted where they clarify ("backbone of industrial workers")
- Policy implications stated clearly in both abstract and conclusion
- "must be noted that" used sparingly for policy-relevant emphasis (NOT for academic claims)

**Abstract structure (≈180–250 words):**
1. Context sentence: broad importance of the topic
2. Problem/Gap sentence: what is unknown or urgent to address
3. Method sentence(s): data source + core identification strategy. Use "is employed as" / "is utilized to" / "is applied to" for method description
4. Results sentences (3–5): substantive findings with directional language ("has a positive effect on," "can enhance," "differs across")
5. Implication sentence: policy relevance or practical takeaway
6. No citations in abstract

**Introduction structure:**
1. Hook paragraph: broad importance, often paired with "not only...but also..." construction
2. Context paragraph: "In the process of [macro trend], [population/region] has experienced [change]..."
3. Problem elaboration (1–2 paragraphs): detailed description of the issue, supported by citations
4. Intervention/mechanism paragraph: introduce the key explanatory variable and its hypothesized role
5. Contribution & roadmap: "The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews... Section 3 introduces... Section 4 describes... Section 5 presents... Section 6 concludes."

**Literature review style:**
- Thematic organization, NOT chronological
- Each paragraph opens with a clear topic sentence defining the sub-literature
- Typical openings: "Several studies have examined...", "Another strand of the literature focuses on...", "The literature has established that..."
- Avoid long individual-paper summaries; group papers by finding/theme
- End by identifying the gap: what the literature does NOT address

**Data section style:**
- "Data from the [survey name] ([acronym]) are used..."
- Chinese-specific institutional details explained parenthetically for international readers: "rural household registration (hukou)"
- All acronyms defined on first mention
- Dependent variables defined with explicit criteria: "X is defined as [criteria]. [Variable] takes the value of 1 if [condition] and 0 otherwise."
- Sample restriction explained step by step: "We restrict the sample to... We further exclude..."

**Method section style:**
- Methods named precisely and described in accessible terms
- Passive voice acceptable for method description: "is employed as the baseline model," "is utilized to address...", "is applied to tackle..."
- Active voice for identification logic: "We exploit [variation] to identify..."
- Each method's purpose stated explicitly: "X is employed as the baseline model, while Y is utilized to address sample selection bias, and Z is applied to tackle endogeneity."

**Results presentation:**
- "Table X presents..." / "Column (1) shows..." / "The results indicate that..."
- Coefficient magnitude stated alongside statistical significance
- "The coefficient on [X] is [magnitude] and statistically significant at the [level] level"
- Null/insignificant results reported honestly: "The positive effect...is insignificant"
- Subgroup analysis: "The effect...differs across [groups]"
- "From a dimensional perspective, [X] can enhance [Y] in terms of [dimension list]"

**Conclusion style:**
- Mirror introduction: restate gap and main findings
- Policy implications scoped to what the evidence supports
- "The research conclusions support the necessity of..."
- "Future research could..." to point to next steps
- No new citations, results, or methodology

### Sentence-Level Conventions

**Preferred constructions:**
| Construction | Example from source paper | Usage note |
|---|---|---|
| "not only...but also..." | "not only closely linked to the well-being of rural residents, but also has a profound influence on rural development" | Paired emphasis; use at most once per section |
| "In the process of..." | "In the process of industrialization and urbanization..." | Contextual framing at paragraph open |
| "...is specifically manifested in the following:" | "...is specifically manifested in the following: while their earnings exceed..." | List precursor; use colon, then explain |
| "are more vulnerable to" | "rural migrant workers are more vulnerable to economic fluctuations" | Comparative disadvantage framing |
| "a situation attributable to" | "a situation attributable to individual factors and the broader socioeconomic structure" | Causal attribution without overclaiming |
| "It must be noted that" | "it must be noted that enhancing nonfarm employment quality requires efforts directed at digital skills" | Policy emphasis ONLY; max once per paper |
| "can enhance" / "can bring" | "Internet usage can enhance the quality of..." | Moderate certainty; stronger than "may" |
| "are used to analyze" | "Data from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) are used to analyze..." | Passive voice for data/methods |

**Transition vocabulary (in order of strength):**
- Additive: "Additionally," → "Moreover," → "Furthermore," (escalating emphasis)
- Contrastive: "However," (mid-sentence, after first clause)
- Causal: "resulting in," "potentially," "a situation attributable to"
- Elaboration: "specifically," "particularly"

**Hedging hierarchy (weak → strong):**
"potentially" → "can" → "are" (no hedge, for established facts) → "must be noted that" (emphasis, rare)

### Prohibited JRS Patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it's wrong for JRS | Replace with |
|---|---|---|
| Overly dense econometric prose in main text | JRS audience is interdisciplinary | Move technical details to footnotes/appendix |
| "We prove that..." | JRS papers demonstrate, not prove | "The results indicate that..." / "Our findings suggest that..." |
| "significant at the 1% level" without magnitude | JRS expects economic/practical significance alongside statistical significance | "A one-unit increase in X is associated with a β increase in Y (p < 0.01)" |
| Rhetorical questions | JRS style is declarative, not Socratic | State the implication directly |
| "This paper is the first to..." | JRS avoids priority claims; let the contribution stand on its own | Frame as gap-filling: "The existing literature has not examined..." |
| Overuse of "we" | Moderate "we" is acceptable (once or twice in methods); avoid "we" in results | Use passive voice or third-person constructions |

### Chinese-Context Conventions (for China-focused JRS papers)

- Chinese-specific institutional terms explained parenthetically on first use: "rural household registration (hukou)"
- Chinese data sources introduced with full name + acronym: "China Family Panel Studies (CFPS)"
- Regional distinctions explained: "In China, individuals with rural household registration (hukou) who engage in nonfarm employment are referred to as rural migrant workers"
- Policy references include both Chinese name and English translation: "the 'Internet+ Employment Training' program"

### Comparison with Other Journals

| Feature | JRS | AER | Econometrica |
|---------|-----|-----|--------------|
| Mathematical density in prose | Low | Medium | High |
| Metaphor use | Occasional, clarifying | Rare | Prohibited |
| Policy framing | Expected | Optional | Rare |
| "must be noted that" | Acceptable (1×) | Rare | Prohibited |
| Audience assumed | Interdisciplinary social scientists | Economists | Economic theorists |
| Chinese-context exposition | Generous | Brief | Brief to none |
| Passive voice in methods | Standard | Minimized | Acceptable |

## General Top-Journal Prose Principles (All Outlets)

### Abstract
- Sentence 1: motivating fact or gap (what is unknown / under-studied)
- Sentence 2: identification strategy or research design
- Sentence 3–4: main finding with quantification
- Sentence 5–6 (optional): mechanism or implication

### Introduction Structure
1. Hook: motivating fact, policy context, or theoretical puzzle (1–2 paragraphs)
2. Gap: what the existing literature cannot tell us (1 paragraph)
3. This paper: what we do, how we identify, what we find (2–4 paragraphs)
4. Contribution: relative to specific related papers (1–2 paragraphs)
5. Roadmap: "The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows..."

### Empirical Results Section
- State the dependent variable and main coefficient of interest at the start of each subsection
- Report: coefficient, standard error, confidence interval, and economic magnitude
- "Column (1) of Table X presents OLS estimates; Column (2) adds [controls]..."
- Every table must be self-contained (clear column headers, footnotes defining variables and SE clustering)

### Robustness Section
- Address threats in decreasing order of plausibility
- Standard structure: "A potential concern is [X]. To address this, we [do Y]. Table [Z] shows..."
- Placebo test: "If our identification strategy is valid, we should find no effect on [placebo outcome]. Column (3) confirms this: the coefficient is [close to zero / statistically insignificant]."

### Conclusion
- No new citations, results, or methodology
- Mirror introduction: restate gap and main finding
- Limitations: 2–4 sentences; be honest but not self-undermining
- Policy implications: scoped to what the evidence supports; avoid overclaiming
